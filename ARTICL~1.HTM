The working-capital questions most boards forget to ask

Every cash crisis I have seen was visible in the numbers a quarter earlier. The problem is rarely the data — it is that nobody asked the question the data would have answered.

Anuruddha Dias  ·  18 July 2026  ·  12 min read

Start here. First paragraph does the work: state the thing you believe that your reader probably does not, and make it specific enough to be wrong. Vague openings read as filler and readers leave.

Second paragraph gives them the reason to keep reading — usually a hint of the evidence, or a promise of what they will be able to do differently by the end.

Why the standard view is incomplete

Body text. Short paragraphs. Concrete nouns. If a sentence could appear in any article on this topic, cut it.

A pull quote is useful roughly once per piece — for the sentence you would want someone to screenshot and put on LinkedIn.

Use a numbered structure when you are giving the reader something to act on:

  1. The first question. What it is, and what a bad answer looks like.
  2. The second question. Same treatment.
  3. The third question. Same again.
  4. The fourth question. And the one people skip.

In practice

Use a callout for the practical takeaway, the caveat, or the "if you only do one thing" line. One or two per article, no more.

What would change my mind

This section is optional and it is the one that builds the most credibility. Saying what evidence would falsify your view marks you as someone worth arguing with rather than someone performing certainty.
